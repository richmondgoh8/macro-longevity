Singapore supermarket guide

Read food labels

Start with the nutrition panel and ingredient list. Compare the amount you’ll actually eat or drink.

The 30-second check

Turn the pack over

  1. Compare like with like. Use per 100 g for similar foods or per 100 mL for similar drinks. Check sugars, sodium and saturated fat, and fibre when it’s listed.
  2. Check the full pack. If you’ll finish it, multiply the per-serving amount by the servings in the pack.
  3. Read the ingredients. They are generally listed heaviest first. Look across the list for several separate sugars or syrups.

Worked example · bottled tea

For drinks in Singapore, use Nutri-Grade to compare sugar and saturated fat. Prefer A or B when shown. A and B labels are optional. Use the nutrition panel for total sugar because the grade subtracts declared lactose and galactose.

“No added sugar” can still contain sugar

What the front of the pack means

  • No added sugar. Naturally present sugars can remain, such as fruit sugars in 100% juice or lactose in plain yoghurt. The claim can also appear on products with sweeteners or sugar alcohols.
  • Unsweetened. Check total sugars for naturally present sources. Under HPB guidance, the claim also excludes added sweeteners and sugar alcohols.
  • Sugar-free. HPB allows up to 0.5 g sugars per 100 g or 100 mL, or up to 0.5 g per serving for meal-type products. Check total carbohydrate if it matters to you. Starch and sugar alcohols can still be present.
  • Reduced sugar. HPB guidance calls for at least 25% less sugar than the reference product. Compare the actual sugar per 100 g or 100 mL on the shelf.
HPB · Nutrition claims

Names to spot in the ingredients

Look for cane sugar, sucrose, dextrose, maltose, glucose or corn syrup, honey, agave, and fruit juice concentrate. Several sweeteners may appear separately lower in the list. Use the nutrition panel to check the total.

WHO counts the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice or concentrate as free sugars. For a regular snack, choose whole fruit over juice.

Maltitol, sorbitol and xylitol are sugar alcohols. Larger amounts can cause digestive symptoms for some people.

Spot ultra-processed foods

Look for signs of industrial formulation

The NOVA system looks at how and why a food was made. Flavourings, colours, emulsifiers, sweeteners, hydrolysed proteins and modified starches can be clues. Use the full product and its nutrition panel to make your choice.

Plain rolled oats, unsweetened frozen berries and canned beans are convenient options. Instant noodle cups, sweetened drinks and many flavoured snack bars are common ultra-processed examples.

NOVA · How to identify ultra-processed foods

Make the biggest swaps first

  • Sweet drinks. Choose water or unsweetened tea more often. In kopi or teh, say kosong and specify no condensed milk if you want it plain.
  • Processed meat. Choose fish, tofu or fresh meat more often than luncheon meat, sausages and bacon. IARC links eating processed meat with colorectal cancer.
  • Salty packaged meals. Compare sodium in soup bases, sauces and instant noodles, and account for the seasoning you use.

Other label traps

Check what the claim leaves out

  • Multigrain or brown bread. Look for wholemeal flour or another whole grain near the top of the list.
  • High protein, plant-based or low fat. Check sugar, sodium, saturated fat and the serving size alongside the featured claim.
  • No added salt or reduced sodium. Check the actual sodium per 100 g or 100 mL. A reduced claim compares with a reference product.
  • Low calorie. Compare with how much you use, especially for sprays, dressings and other products with tiny serving sizes.
  • Uncured or no added nitrates. On some imported deli meats, check for a qualifier about naturally occurring nitrates or nitrites from celery. Treat deli meat as processed meat.

Read nutrition badges in context

For everyday nutrition, compare the Healthier Choice Symbol within the same category. Check the panel for the nutrients that matter to you.

Trans fat-free describes one type of fat. Still check saturated fat. Singapore prohibits partially hydrogenated oils in food sold locally.

Food and packaging safety

Act on allergens and recalls

If you have a food allergy, check the ingredients and allergen information each time. If a recall matches your batch, stop using the product and follow SFA’s instructions.

Suspect a severe allergic reaction? Use the person’s adrenaline auto-injector if available and call 995 immediately. Signs can include difficulty breathing, throat swelling or collapse.

SFA · Labels and allergens NHG · Anaphylaxis SFA · Recall notices

Match the container to the heat

For the microwave, use a container marked microwave-safe and follow its instructions. Transfer food from takeaway tubs without reheating instructions into a microwave-safe dish. Use containers designed for repeated use when storing meals regularly.

SFA · Food packaging

Choose lower-mercury fish more often

Rotate choices such as salmon and sardines. For most adults, limit large predatory fish such as swordfish, shark, king mackerel and bigeye tuna.

During pregnancy, avoid these predatory fish and continue choosing lower-mercury fish. Use the age-specific chart when choosing fish for children.

Microplastics · evidence still developing

Researchers have detected microplastics in foods and drinks. The FDA says available evidence has yet to establish a health risk at the levels found in food. Exposure estimates and long-term effects remain uncertain. Follow the packaging guidance above when reheating or storing food.

Suspected mercury or heavy-metal exposure

What to do after suspected exposure

Call 995 in Singapore for breathing difficulty, a seizure or loss of consciousness.

  1. Stop the exposure. Stop using the suspect product. If metallic mercury spills, leave the area, keep others away and avoid vacuuming or sweeping, which can spread it.
  2. Contact a doctor promptly. Share what you were exposed to, when and for how long. Take a photo of the product label if it is safe to do so. The clinician can choose the right test for the type and timing of exposure.
  3. Skip home “detox” products. Over-the-counter chelation can cause serious harm. Prescription chelation is reserved for selected cases under specialist supervision.
