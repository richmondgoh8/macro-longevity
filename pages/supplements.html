Before you buy

  1. Check the seller Buy from an established pharmacy or retailer. Pass on claims of rapid results or disease cures.
  2. Read the serving size Check the active ingredient, amount per serving and how many capsules make a serving.
  3. Check for overlap Compare ingredients across products. Bring the labels to your pharmacist if you use regular medicines or several supplements.
HSA · Choosing health supplements

When each supplement is useful

Before and during early pregnancy

Folic acid

Start before conception and continue through the first 12 weeks of pregnancy to lower the risk of neural tube defects.

Check the dose with your clinic. Singapore MOH notes 400 micrograms (0.4 mg) daily is generally sufficient for low-risk pregnancies. Your clinic may prescribe a different dose based on your history.

KKH · Pregnancy guidance MOH · Folic acid dosing

When intake is low

Vitamin D3

Vitamin D helps your body absorb calcium. Supplementation is useful when intake is inadequate or you have a diagnosed deficiency. Most healthy adults under 75 can focus on meeting the recommended intake.

Check the label. Look at the dose per serving. For vitamin D, 1 microgram equals 40 IU. Ask your clinician before taking high doses or treating a low test result.

NIH · Vitamin D Endocrine Society guideline

Food first

Omega-3

Oily fish such as sardines and salmon provide EPA and DHA. If you rarely eat fish, ask whether fish oil or an algae-based supplement makes sense for you.

Check the EPA and DHA amounts per serving. Ask your clinician about prescription treatment for high triglycerides. Check before taking high-dose fish oil or combining it with blood thinners.

NIH · Omega-3 evidence AHA · Triglyceride treatment

Optional, depending on your goal

For strength and power training

Creatine monohydrate

Monohydrate is the best-studied form for strength training and repeated short bursts of effort. A common adult sports protocol uses 3–5 g daily, with loading optional.

Tell your clinic if you take it. Creatine can raise your blood creatinine reading. Ask your clinician before starting if you have kidney disease.

Australian Institute of Sport · Creatine Creatine and kidney blood tests

Limited evidence for sleep

Magnesium glycinate

Magnesium supplements can help address low intake when needed. A short trial of bisglycinate found a small improvement in reported insomnia symptoms.

Check elemental magnesium per serving. Ask a pharmacist about interactions with medicines such as some antibiotics. Get medical advice before taking it if you have kidney disease.

NIH · Magnesium safety Bisglycinate sleep trial

Tea and supplements for sleep

If sleep problems keep coming back, ask your GP about cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).

  • Chamomile tea. A caffeine-free option for winding down. Evidence for treating insomnia is limited. Avoid it if you’re allergic to chamomile or related plants such as ragweed. Check with a pharmacist if you take warfarin or sedatives.
  • Glycine. Small, short studies report better perceived sleep. Evidence is too limited to recommend it for chronic insomnia.
  • Taurine. Human evidence is insufficient to recommend it for insomnia.

Psyllium husk

Psyllium, also called ispaghula husk, is an optional soluble fibre supplement for bowel regularity. Trials also found modest reductions in LDL cholesterol among adults with elevated cholesterol.

  • Mix it with water. Use the amount of water specified on the label and drink promptly after mixing. Swallowing it dry or with too little water can cause choking.
  • Check whether it suits you. Avoid it if you have trouble swallowing. Ask a pharmacist first if you have a history of bowel blockage, a fluid restriction, or take oral medicines. The pharmacist can advise how far apart to take them.
